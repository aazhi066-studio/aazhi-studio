Around 1310, King Maravarman Kulasekhara Pandya had ruled Madurai for about forty years. Two Persian-language chroniclers of the time, Wassaf and Amir Khusrau, say he favoured his son Vira Pandya, whom they call illegitimate, over his son Sundara Pandya, and that Sundara killed his father and seized the throne. A civil war between the brothers followed. Some accounts say Sundara went north to the Delhi Sultanate for help. In 1311 an army of the Delhi Sultanate under Malik Kafur marched into the Pandya country; Amir Khusrau, a Delhi court poet, wrote that Vira's capital was found empty, and claimed great plunder, including hundreds of elephants. But the story is contested: some later writers name Vira as the killer, and some historians think the raid was a campaign for loot with no link to the brothers' feud. AAZHI, the Tamil chronicles.

SOURCES AND PROOF
1. Maravarman Kulasekara Pandyan I (Wikipedia) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maravarman_Kulasekara_Pandyan_I
   Supports: his long reign (from 1268 to about 1308-1310); the succession dispute between his sons Sundara Pandya and Vira Pandya; that Wassaf and Amir Khusrau say Sundara killed him (dated 1310); the civil war between the brothers.
2. Malik Kafur's invasion of the Pandya kingdom (Wikipedia) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malik_Kafur%27s_invasion_of_the_Pandya_kingdom
   Supports: the 1310-1311 campaign of the Delhi Sultanate's general Malik Kafur into the Pandya country; Wassaf's account of Kulasekhara favouring Vira, whom he calls illegitimate; that some chroniclers say Sundara sought Delhi's help; Amir Khusrau's account that Vira's capital was found deserted and that Madurai was raided; the large and varying numbers of elephants and treasure claimed (counts differ by source).
3. Epigraphia Indica (Archaeological Survey of India), discussion of the later Pandya succession and Wassaf's account (via jainqq.org scans), and K. A. Nilakanta Sastri, The Pandyan Kingdom (1929)
   Supports: Wassaf's statement that the preference for the illegitimate Vira led Sundara to kill his father; the scholarly view that the 1310-1311 campaign was a raid for loot not connected to the princes' rivalry.

WHAT IS ILLUSTRATION
The whole story is shown as a game on a carved chessboard. The gold king, ivory and ebony princes and grey army pieces are symbols, not portraits; no real image of any of these people exists or is shown. The marbled ink and the parchment pages are decoration: the pages show abstract lines, not real text from any chronicle. Numbers on screen are the claims of the sources, not counts.

WHAT IS UNCERTAIN
- Who killed Kulasekhara is contested. The Persian chroniclers name Sundara; some later accounts and summaries name Vira; Tamil records do not settle it.
- Dates differ: his death is given as 1308 or 1310, and the civil war as 1308 to 1323.
- Whether Sundara asked Delhi for help, and whether that caused the 1311 raid, is debated; some historians see the raid as a campaign for loot with no link to the feud.
- Amir Khusrau was a court poet praising his patron; his numbers of elephants and treasure vary between sources and may be exaggerated.
- "Illegitimate" is the chroniclers' word, not a settled fact.
