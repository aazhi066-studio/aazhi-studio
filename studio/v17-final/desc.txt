For about three centuries after roughly 250 CE, records of the old Tamil kingdoms, the Cheras, Cholas and Pandyas, grow thin. In their place stand rulers called the Kalabhras, whose origin nobody can name for sure. Most of what we know comes from their enemies: the Velvikudi copper-plate grant, issued by the Pandya king Nedunjadaiyan about two centuries later (c. 770 CE), calls the Kalabhra a "Kali king" who drove out countless great kings, and says the Pandya Kadungon took the land back, late in the 500s CE. Historians long called this the "dark age" of Tamil history. Some scholars now argue that the Kalabhras may have backed Jain and Buddhist monks and that Tamil writing may have kept growing in these years. Wreckers, or kings written out by the winners? AAZHI, the Tamil chronicles.

SOURCES AND PROOF
1. Kalabhra dynasty (Wikipedia) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalabhra_dynasty
   Supports: Kalabhra rule over all or parts of the Tamil region sometime between the 3rd and 6th centuries CE, the "dark age" / interregnum label, unknown origins (Kulke and Rothermund: "nothing is known about the origins"), Burton Stein's view of possible Buddhist and Jain backing, and the end of the period under Pallava Simhavishnu and the Pandya Kadungon by the late 6th century.
2. Velvikudi inscription (Wikipedia) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velvikudi_inscription
   Supports: the 8th-century Pandya copper-plate grant of Nedunjadaiyan (c. 769-770 CE) renewing the Velvikudi village grant; its account of a Kali king, Kalabhran, who drove away numberless great kings, and of Kadungon taking the throne; that it was written at least 200 years after Kalabhra rule and is legendary and exaggerated.
3. "The Kalabhras in the Pandiya country" (Tamil Digital Library) https://www.tamildigitallibrary.in/admin/assets/book/TVA_BOK_0019058_The_Kalabhras_in_the_Pandiya_country.pdf
   Supports: a scholarly study of the Kalabhra period in the Pandya country and the debate about it.

WHAT IS ILLUSTRATION
Everything on screen is drawn as hammered copper plates and black ink. Kings, monks and the Kalabhra ruler are faceless silhouettes; nobody knows what any of them looked like. The marks on the plates are abstract dashes, unreadable on purpose: no real inscription text is shown or quoted. The map and the emblems (bow, tiger, fish) are simplified symbols.

WHAT IS UNCERTAIN
- Dates are approximate: sources give roughly the 3rd to the 6th century CE (some say up to the 7th). "Three hundred years" is a round figure.
- Who the Kalabhras were and where they came from is unknown and debated.
- The Velvikudi plates were written by the Kalabhras' enemies about two centuries later; their account is partly legendary and may be exaggerated.
- Jain and Buddhist patronage and a growth of Tamil writing in this period are scholars' arguments, not settled facts.
- "Dark age" is a label used by historians, and many now dispute it.
