Can a poem kill a king? A Tamil legend says one did. The story goes that the Pallava king Nandivarman III (9th century CE) defeated his half-brothers in a fight for the throne, and one of them answered with words: a poem praising the king, the Nandi Kalambakam, with a curse (aram) hidden in its verses. The legend says the king sat to hear it under a row of pavilions, each one catching fire as a verse ended, until with the last verse the king burned too. The record is different: the poem is real and praises a real Nandivarman, the victor at Tellaru, but no inscription tells of a cursed death, and scholars doubt that a brother wrote it. Murder by poetry, or a story to explain how a king died? Tell us in the comments. AAZHI, the Tamil chronicles.

SOURCES AND PROOF
1. Nandivarman III (Wikipedia) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nandivarman_III
   Supports: Nandivarman III was a 9th-century Pallava king (reign given as about 846-869 CE), his victory at Tellaru, and that the Nandi Kalambakam is a Tamil poem about him.
2. நந்திக் கலம்பகம் (Nandi Kalambakam), Tamil Wiki https://tamil.wiki/wiki/நந்திக்_கலம்பகம்
   Supports: the traditional story that a brother who could not win in war composed the kalambakam with "aram" verses to kill the king, the burning-pavilion tale, that it is a legend which need not be historical, and that the author and date of the poem are not known for certain.
3. Kalanjiyam Tamil research journal, article on the history and legends of the Nandi Kalambakam https://ngmtamil.in/kalanjiyam-tamil/index.php/kalanjiyam/article/view/265
   Supports: the legend of the half-brothers (sons of the king's father's second wife) who fought the king, lost, and then had the poem composed with aram verses that led to his death; and that this is tradition.
4. Older scholarship on the poem (discussed in the Indian Antiquary and later studies): the poem is commonly ascribed to a younger brother of the king, but the way the author speaks of himself makes that unlikely.

WHAT IS ILLUSTRATION
Everything on screen is drawn as etched palm-leaf manuscript art. The king, the brothers, the reader, the pavilions, the battle and the fire are stylised, faceless illustrations; no real portrait or manuscript is shown. The verse lines on the leaves and the marks on the tablet are abstract and unreadable on purpose: they are not the real text of the poem or of any inscription.

WHAT IS UNCERTAIN
- The cursed poem, the burning pavilions and the king's death by fire are LEGEND, not record. No inscription supports them.
- Who wrote the Nandi Kalambakam is not known; the brother-author story is doubted by scholars.
- The exact reign dates of Nandivarman III vary between sources (roughly 825-869 CE are given), and how and when he died is not recorded in detail.
- "Aram" here means verses believed to carry the power to harm; whether the legend first arose to explain the king's death is a guess.
