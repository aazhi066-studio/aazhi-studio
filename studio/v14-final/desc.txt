Around 1656, Madurai and Mysore fought a war remembered not for its battles but for noses. Tirumala Nayak of Madurai had sided with Mysore's enemy, the Sultan of Bijapur; when Bijapur's army left, Kanthirava Narasaraja of Mysore sent his army south under the general Hampaiya. Old accounts say his soldiers cut off the noses of captives, even villagers, and sent them to Srirangapatna as trophies. Tirumala, old and ill, called on Raghunatha Sethupathi of Ramnad, whose army drove the invaders back; Madurai then struck into Mysore country, and accounts say it took noses too. AAZHI, the Tamil chronicles.

SOURCES AND PROOF
1. Madurai–Mysore Wars (Wikipedia) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madurai%E2%80%93Mysore_Wars
   Supports: Tirumala Nayaka's alliance with Bijapur against Mysore; Kanthirava Narasaraja I's punitive invasion of Madurai around 1656 after Bijapur's forces withdrew; the Mysore army under Dalavayi Hampaiya; the nose-cutting of captives including civilians and the noses sent to Srirangapatna (Seringapatam); Tirumala calling on Raghunatha Sethupathi; the Madurai counter-attack into Mysore territory with similar cruelty.
2. Battle of Madurai (1656) (Wikipedia) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Madurai_(1656)
   Supports: the commanders on each side (Tirumala Nayaka and Raghunatha Sethupathi against Kanthirava Narasaraja I), the Ramnad army given as 25,000, and a Madurai victory.
3. R. Sathyanatha Aiyar, History of the Nayaks of Madura (Oxford University Press, 1924), Internet Archive https://archive.org/details/historyofthenaya035252mbp
   Supports: the standard account of the Mysore invasion, the "hunt for noses", the Sethupathi's help, the Madurai reprisal and the claim about the Mysore king's nose.

   Supports: Tirumala Nayak as ruler of Madurai and his death around 1659.

WHAT IS ILLUSTRATION
Everything on screen is painted in the style of round ganjifa playing cards. The kings, the general, soldiers, the palace and the forts are stylised, faceless illustrations, not portraits. The cruelty is shown only by symbols (sacks, a blank face whose nose is painted over); nothing is a real image.

WHAT IS UNCERTAIN
- The nose-cutting is described in old chronicles and later histories; details such as numbers, and exactly who did what to whom, vary between accounts.
- That the noses were sent to Srirangapatna in sacks comes from those accounts and cannot be checked independently.
- The claim that the Mysore king lost his own nose comes from a Madurai-side account and is widely treated as legend.
- The size of the Ramnad army (25,000) is a traditional figure.
- Dates are approximate: the invasion is placed around 1655-1656 and Tirumala's death around 1659.
