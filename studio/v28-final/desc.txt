In the Sangam age, Pari, a Velir chief, ruled Parambu hill, and the old Tamil poems praise him for giving its villages away to poets. A famous legend says he even gave his chariot to a jasmine creeper that had nothing to climb. Then the Chera, Chola and Pandya kings joined together and besieged his hill. His friend, the poet Kapilar, warned them that armies would never take it, though singers only had to ask. Yet the hill fell and Pari was killed. How he died is not told in the old poems, and later tellings disagree. Envy of a generous man, or a war for his hill? AAZHI, the Tamil chronicles.

   Supports: Pari, Velir chief of Parambu (Parambu nadu, about 300 villages), famed for generosity, the jasmine creeper and chariot legend, the joint attack of the Chera, Chola and Pandya kings, his death, and his daughters Angavai and Sangavai.

   Supports: Kapilar, Sangam poet and friend of Pari, sang of him in the Purananuru, told the besieging kings the hill could not be taken by force, became guardian of Pari's daughters and sought husbands for them among other chiefs.
3. Purananuru, poems 105-120 (Kapilar on Pari) and 112 (the lament credited to Pari's daughters, the two full moons); 200-202 (Kapilar approaching other chiefs). English translation: George L. Hart and Hank Heifetz, "The Four Hundred Songs of War and Wisdom" (Columbia University Press, 1999).

WHAT IS ILLUSTRATION
Everything on screen is batik-style wax and dye artwork. Pari, the kings, Kapilar, the daughters, Avvaiyar, the hill, the chariot, the moon and the armies are illustrations; no real portrait, place or object is shown. The lines on the poem leaves are abstract and are not real text.

WHAT IS UNCERTAIN
- Sangam-age dates are uncertain (often placed in the early centuries CE); Pari's exact years are unknown.
- The chariot and jasmine creeper story is a legend.
- Why the three kings attacked is not clearly stated in the early poems; later retellings give different reasons.
- HOW Pari died is DISPUTED: the early poems mourn his death without describing it; later tellings speak of treachery and do not agree on the details.
- Poem 112 is credited by tradition to Pari's daughters; authorship of such poems is traditional, not proven.
- That the poet Avvaiyar later helped the daughters marry (often named as marrying into the Malaiyaman family near Tirukkovilur) comes from later tradition, and accounts differ.
